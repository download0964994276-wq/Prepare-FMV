Financial Management & Corporate Valuation

1. Core Financial Foundations & Management

1. The Concepts of Time Value of Money (TVM)

A dollar today is worth more than a dollar in the future because today's dollar can earn interest or returns over time.

  • Present Value (PV): Current value of a future sum.
  • Future Value (FV): Value of a current asset at a specified future date.
  • Discounting / Compounding: Moving cash flows backward to present value vs. earning interest forward.

2. Current Ratio

Measures a company's ability to cover its short-term obligations (due within 1 year) with short-term assets.

Current Ratio = Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities

Interpretation: A ratio $> 1.0$ indicates that the company has more current assets than short-term liabilities (good liquidity).

3. Book Value vs. Market Value

Book Value: Historical accounting value based on balance sheet costs minus depreciation.

Market Value: Current trading price in the open market reflecting forward-looking expectations.

Key Difference: Book value looks backward at historical accounting costs; Market value looks forward, reflecting current market expectations and future cash-flow potential.

4. Intrinsic Value Based on Future Cash Flows

The true economic value of an asset calculated as the present value of all expected future cash flows.

- Rule: If Market Price < Intrinsic Value : Undervalued. If Market Price > Intrinsic Value : Overvalued.

5. Concept of Shareholder Wealth Maximization

The fundamental objective of corporate financial management is to maximize long-term shareholder value(represented by the stock price).

- Why focus on stock price? Stock prices reflect current performance as well as market expectations regarding future profitability, risk, and growth.

6. Primary Goal of Management in Finance

Goal: To maximize the current value per share of existing stock (or maximize long-term shareholder wealth).

Secondary Goals: Increasing market share, boosting revenue, or cutting costs are only useful if they serve the ultimate goal of enhancing shareholder value without creating excessive risk.

7. Agency Problem

Conflict of interest between principals (shareholders) and agents (managers).

Agency Costs: Costs incurred to align managers' goals with shareholders' goals (e.g., performance bonuses, stock options, auditing fees, or board oversight).

8. Financial Management, Investment, and International Management

  • Financial Management: Decisions on acquiring assets (investing) and raising funds (financing) to maximize firm value.
  • Investment Management: Managing portfolios of securities, assessing risk/return profiles, and executing asset allocation.
  • International Management: Managing financial decisions across foreign currencies, international tax rules, political risks, and global capital markets.

9. Net Income vs. Cash Flows

Net Income: Accounting profit shown on the Income Statement calculated using accrual accounting (Revenue earned $-$ Expenses incurred). Includes non-cash expenses like depreciation.

Cash Flows: Actual cash coming in (inflow) and going out (outflow).

Key Rule: Net Income does not equal cash. A company can be profitable on paper (high Net Income) but run out of cash if receivables are uncollected.

10. Stockholder vs. Bondholder

Feature Stockholder (Equity) Bondholder (Debt)
Role Partial owner of the firm Creditor / Lender of the firm
Return Dividends & Capital Gains (Variable) Fixed Coupon Interest
Risk & Priority Higher risk (residual claim) Lower risk (priority claim)
Voting Rights Yes No

11. The Study of Financial Management

Focuses on three main managerial decisions:

  • Capital Budgeting: Which long-term investments/projects should the firm undertake?
  • Capital Structure: How should the firm finance these investments (Debt vs. Equity)?
  • Working Capital Management: How does the firm manage day-to-day short-term operating assets and liabilities?

2. Capital Structure, Risk & Return

12. Cost of Debt vs. Cost of Equity

Cost of Debt (r_d or K_d): The effective rate a company pays on its borrowed funds (bonds, loans). It is lower than equity because debt has priority in liquidation and offers tax benefits.

Cost of Equity (r_e or K_e): The return required by equity investors to compensate for taking on business and financial risk. Equity is non-tax-deductible and more expensive.

12.1. Cost of Debt is Tax Shields

Concept: Interest payments on debt are tax-deductible, creating an interest tax shield that lowers the firm's tax bill.

After-Tax Cost of Debt = r_d × (1 - Tax Rate)

13. CAPM Only Forecasting Systematic Risks (Beta)

CAPM explains the relationship between systematic risk and expected return, determining the Cost of Equity (Re) for WACC.

E(Ri) = Rf + Beta × [ E(Rm) - Rf ]
  • Rf (Risk-Free Rate): Return on zero-default-risk government bonds.
  • Beta (β): Measures sensitivity to overall market movements (systematic risk).
  • Rm (Market Return): Expected return of the overall market.
  • [Rm - Rf] (Market Risk Premium): Extra return required for taking market risk.

14. Systematic Risk vs. Unsystematic Risk

Systematic Risk (Market / Non-diversifiable Risk): Risk factors that affect the entire market (e.g., inflation, interest rate changes, recessions, wars). Measured by Beta ($\beta$). Cannot be eliminated by diversification.

Unsystematic Risk (Firm-specific / Diversifiable Risk): Risk unique to a single company or industry (e.g., labor strikes, lawsuits, CEO resignation). Can be eliminated through diversification.

15 & 15.1. WACC (Weighted Average Cost of Capital)

WACC is the average rate of return a company must earn on its investments to satisfy all providers of capital (Debt, Preferred Stock, and Common Equity).

Full Formula (3 Components):

WACC = [ (D/V) × (1 - Tc) × r_debt ] + [ (P/V) × r_preferred ] + [ (E/V) × r_equity ]

Standard Formula (Debt & Equity Only):

WACC = (E/V × Re) + [ (D/V) × Rd × (1 - T) ]
  • V = D + P + E (Total Capital Value)

Relationship Between CAPM and WACC:

  • CAPM calculates the Cost of Equity (r_e), which represents the equity component inside the WACC formula.
  • WACC aggregates both the Cost of Equity (via CAPM) and the After-Tax Cost of Debt according to the company's capital structure weights.

3. Valuation, Rates & NPV

16. Net Present Value (NPV)

The difference between the present value of future cash inflows and the initial outlay/cost.

NPV = Σ CF_t / (1 + t)t

Decision Rule:

  • NPV > 0 : Accept project (adds value to the firm).
  • NPV < 0 : Reject project (destroys value).

17. Expected Rate of Return, Standard Deviation, and Risk-Free Rate

Expected Rate of Return: The weighted average of all possible returns based on their probability of occurrence.

Standard Deviation (σ): A statistical measure of total risk or volatility around expected returns.

Risk-Free Rate (R_f): The return on an investment with zero default risk (typically long-term government bonds, such as U.S. Treasury bills).

18. Nominal Rate vs. Real Rate vs. Risk Premium

Nominal Rate: The stated or quoted interest rate unadjusted for inflation.

Formula (Fisher Effect / Component breakdown):

Nominal Rate = Risk-Free Rate (real rate + inflation rate) + Risk Premium
  • Real Rate: Compensation for postponing consumption (time value of money without inflation).
  • Risk Premium: Additional return required by investors for taking on additional default, liquidity, or market risk.

19. Undervalued or Overvalued Compare with Security Market Line (SML)

Security Market Line (SML): A graphical representation of CAPM plotting Expected Return against Beta (β).

  • Above SML: Undervalued (offers higher return than required for its risk).
  • Below SML: Overvalued (offers lower return than required for its risk).
  • On the SML: Fairly priced.
